# Key takeaways: bridging adults, mentoring and opportunity (eleven projects)

> Exploratory research on public data, 2026-11. Most results are associations, not causal
> effects. **Every figure needs checking against its original source** before it appears in
> funder reports, grant applications or public material.

## The one-sentence version

Young people do better where relationships cross class lines, and those relationships are
scarcest exactly where they would matter most. Mentoring that deliberately bridges, connecting
youth to people, careers and colleges beyond their circles, is the most promising lever. But
its effects are small, it is rarely measured well, and it is nearly absent in the highest-need
places.

## What we expected, and what we found

| We expected... | Verdict | What the data showed |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-class connections go with upward mobility | **True** | Strongest single correlate across about 3,000 counties: +2.7 income percentiles for low-income children per SD of economic connectedness, holding within states (01) |
| More youth nonprofits mean more mobility | **False** | No relationship once other county traits are known. Having organizations is not the same as building connections (01) |
| Supportive non-family adults matter, and access is unequal | **True** | Linked to +14 points flourishing and +12 school engagement. 80% of children below poverty have one vs. 94% above 400% of poverty (02) |
| Mentoring helps disadvantaged youth more | **Mixed** | Yes for school engagement; no for hope and emotional outcomes, where hardship dampens the benefit (02) |
| Confidence is how supportive adults help | **True for school** | Supportive teachers → self-efficacy → achievement in all 65 PISA systems. Belonging is a separate channel, tied to wellbeing (02) |
| Bridging matters at every stage | **Partly** | Strong in high school (4x larger link in mixed schools). At college, friending bias showed no link; access to mixed, well-resourced campuses matters more (02, 04) |
| Low-income teens get less time with adults | **True, but small** | 8 fewer minutes a day with non-family adults, about one hour a week (05) |
| More school counselors reduce chronic absence | **False** | No detectable change over two years. Spreading staff thinner is not the same as a consistent personal relationship (06) |
| Mentoring programs have large effects | **Overstated** | The stronger the study, the weaker the result: 84% of findings positive in weak studies vs. 56% in rigorous ones. Credible effects are about 0.1-0.27 SD. Randomized evidence on graduation is thin and mostly null in K-12 (08) |
| Mentoring pays for itself | **Plausible, unproven** | At $2,000 per youth, breaking even needs about 1 extra graduate per 100. That's inside the credible range, but no strong study confirms it (07, 08) |
| Bridging programs work better | **Promising lead** | They stay more positive within rigor levels (79% vs. 52%, few studies). Nonprofits that mentor are 10x as likely to bridge (21% vs. 2%) (08, 10) |
| Federal youth money reaches high-need places | **False for STEM; true for college access** | NSF youth STEM: highest-need counties get 0.46x their per-child share. College-access programs: 1.40x. Priority counties are nearly absent (09) |
| Federal STEM investment favors mentoring | **False** | AI-focused awards rose from 2% to 21% (2014-24). Mentoring stayed at about 14% (3% central). Rigorous evaluation plans fell from 17% to 8% (09) |
| Mentoring supply is thin | **Mixed** | Nationally about 5x more mentoring than IRS codes show (12.7% vs. 2.3% of youth organizations). But only 4 of 124 priority counties have a filing mentoring organization in the county (10) |
| Programs measure their results | **False** | 1.3% of youth nonprofits report numbered outcomes; 76% claim no outcome at all (10) |
| AI threatens low-income youth pathways | **Mostly false, one big exception** | AI can do much of 19% of jobs open without a bachelor's vs. 39% of degree jobs. The exception is the office "first rung": about 17M jobs (customer service, administrative assistants, clerks) (11) |
| Priority counties' economies are exposed to AI | **False** | Less exposed than elsewhere (30% vs. 35%) because local work is hands-on, care and service (11) |
| AI agents can do this research at scale | **True, with checks** | Agreement with a second model of kappa 0.78-0.96. Correlation with human labels r = 0.80. Skimming, shifted rows and one shortcut batch were caught and fixed (08-11) |

## What this means for a program (for example, AI4ALL)

1. **Where:** start in or near the priority counties and partner across county lines, since
   local mentoring supply is nearly zero. Federal STEM money won't fill the gap.
2. **What:** mentoring that bridges, through career mentors, introductions and college and
   credential pathways. About one hour a week matches the gap.
3. **Toward which jobs:** human-anchored credentials (skilled trades, allied health, utilities).
   Certificates pay Pell completers $50-69K in jobs AI can't do. Treat office entry jobs as
   stepping stones, and teach AI as a tool in every pathway.
4. **How to know it works:**
   - Plan for small effects (about 400 youth per group).
   - Measure confidence and belonging separately.
   - Link to graduation records with consent.
   - Use a lottery when oversubscribed.
   - Protect the comparison group from dropping out.
5. **What to claim:** the rigorous evidence supports modest, specific promises, not
   transformation. Weak designs overstate impact.

## Still open

- Whether bridging mentoring causally raises graduation or earnings: it needs a randomized pilot.
- Local reality in priority counties, including churches, schools and informal mentors that no
  dataset sees.
- Human audits of the AI-coded datasets (08-11) and source checks of every figure before public
  use.
